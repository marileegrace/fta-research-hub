Why the stock fell, and why we think the reaction went too far

On November 5, Five9 reported Q3 revenue of $323.3M (+13.1% YoY). That was above the top of its own $316–322M guidance range and its strongest growth in two years. Non-GAAP EPS was $0.82 against a $0.80 consensus. Even so, shares fell from about $34 to $27.40, roughly 20%. Three things drove the selloff. Full-year EPS guidance was cut to $3.19 from $3.26, because a planned restructuring was pushed into 2027 and the share count came in higher. Management agreed to buy Level AI for $325M in cash, about 16× its ~$20M of current ARR, a deal it says will cut 2027 EBITDA margin by about 1 point. And the CFO warned that strong AI ramps will make 2027 year-over-year comparisons hard.

All three are real, but they are about the next few quarters, not the next several years. The underlying trend is what decides the stock over 3–5 years, and it is clearly improving. Subscription revenue grew 12% → 13% → 14% → 16% over the last four quarters. Subscription retention went from 105% to 109%. A ~$100M TCV win was announced in Q2. RPO reached $1.6B. Five9 also retired about 3.5M shares at $25.97 in its September ASR, which tells us management sees value close to today's price.

The non-consensus insight

The common view is that AI agents shrink the number of human agents in contact centers, and so they shrink a seat-priced vendor's revenue. That holds for a company that only sells seats. Five9 is moving the other way. Its AI products (Agent Assist, Genius Routing, Voice AI Agents) are priced on usage and outcomes, and Level AI adds a standalone AI layer that can be sold to contact centers running competitors' platforms, including on-premise ones. The real TAM is the labor budget, not the software budget, and labor spending in contact centers is many times larger than CCaaS software spending. If Five9 captures even a small slice of the labor it automates, revenue per customer can rise while seat counts fall. Today's price leaves almost nothing for that outcome.

Asymmetry in one lineAt 7× EBITDA with about $700M of cash and investments, Five9 is valued like a business in decline. The bear case (~$19) is down about 32%; the base case (~$34) is up about 21%; the bull case (~$45, or ~$80 over 3 years) is up 60–185%. Five9 also has takeover appeal: Zoom offered about $14.7B for it in 2021, and private equity has already taken out a peer (Verint, Thoma Bravo, 2025).

Microeconomic & Valuation Analysis

▾

Industry & TAM

Five9 sells cloud contact-center software (CCaaS). This is the system that routes customer calls, chats and messages to human or AI agents, records and scores them, and connects to CRMs like Salesforce and ServiceNow. Grand View Research projects the CCaaS market will reach about $17.1B by 2030 at an 18.4% CAGR. Research & Markets puts 2025–2030 growth at about 16.5%. Cloud penetration of the roughly 17M-seat global agent base is still incomplete, and much of the enterprise market still runs on-premise Avaya, Cisco and Genesys systems. That installed base is the replacement pool for every CCaaS vendor.

The bigger opportunity sits next to CCaaS: AI for customer experience. NICE reported AI ARR of $392M (+46%), and Five9's AI business runs at roughly $180M ARR (+91%). Hyperscalers (Google CES, AWS Connect, Microsoft) and AI-native startups (Sierra, Decagon, Cresta) are all competing for the same automation budget. We think the right way to frame the market is that CCaaS becomes the system of record and orchestration layer, while AI becomes the revenue layer on top.

Qualitative Analysis — Moat, Management & Culture

▾

The moat, viewed as a system

A contact center is the nervous system of a company's customer relationships. It ties together telephony carriers, CRM data, workforce scheduling, compliance recording (PCI, HIPAA, TCPA), quality scoring, and now AI models. Replacing it means re-plumbing hundreds of integrations and retraining thousands of agents, and a bad cutover can mean dropped calls during peak season. Five9's moat is therefore mostly about switching costs and operational embedding, not network effects. That shows in its LTM subscription retention, which sits above 100% even during a weak 2025.

The moat is narrower than it was in 2020. CCaaS has partly become a commodity: NICE CXone, Genesys Cloud, Talkdesk, AWS Connect, and Zoom Contact Center all offer comparable routing. Where we think a new moat is forming is in data gravity. Every interaction Five9 handles produces a labeled dataset (intent, outcome, agent action, sentiment). That dataset makes AI routing and Agent Assist better, which brings in more AI volume. Level AI's QA and analytics engine strengthens this loop by letting Five9 collect interaction data from contact centers that don't run Five9's own platform. That is the real strategic logic behind the acquisition. It is a purchase of data reach more than of revenue.

Moat scorecardSwitching costs: Strong · Data and AI flywheel: Emerging · Scale vs. NICE and Genesys: Weaker (about 40% of NICE's revenue) · Brand in the mid-market: Strong · Ecosystem (Google, Salesforce, ServiceNow; over 80% of business partner-influenced): Good, but dependent on partners.

Founder-led? Management & board

Five9 is no longer founder-led. Long-time CEO Mike Burkland, who built the company from a $13 IPO in 2014 to about $1.1B of revenue, stepped back to the board. Amit Mathradas, formerly CEO of workflow-automation company Nintex, was named CEO late in 2025 and took over in February 2026. Since taking over he has rebuilt the leadership team: a new CTO (Niranjan Vijayaragavan), a new Chief Sales Officer (Robert Hornish), an EVP of Transformation & Strategy, and a new Chief Marketing & Growth Officer. CFO Bryan Lee was promoted from interim CFO in July 2025. So many changes at once are a risk to execution, but the growth pickup through 2026 has happened on the new team's watch.

Capital allocation

The record is mixed but getting better. On the positive side, the company repaid its 2025 convertible notes in cash, bought back about $150M of stock in the last 12 months (including the $90M ASR at an average of $25.97), approved a new $200M program, and kept diluted share count roughly flat (≈76.4M in Q2'26 vs. ≈76.7M in Q2'25). On the negative side, Level AI at about 16× trailing ARR is expensive, and past acquisitions (Inference, Virtual Observer, Aceyus) did not clearly speed up growth. The $747.5M of 1% convertible notes due 2029 leaves net cash slightly negative before the deal and about −$370M after it closes, which is still easy to manage at roughly 1.2× EBITDA.

Culture & organizational traits

  • Customer obsession: Five9 has historically ranked highly on Gartner Peer Insights and G2 for mid-market service. The open-ecosystem approach (letting customers bring their own AI partners) puts customer choice ahead of lock-in.
  • Innovation pace: It has picked up: Voice AI Agents launched in Q2'26, Genius Routing is scaling, and an agentic CX roadmap was laid out. Management says Level AI pulls the roadmap forward by about 12 months, which also admits that internal R&D was falling behind.
  • Failure tolerance: Moderate. The new CEO started open "portfolio deep dives" and has said he is willing to cut products, which suggests a culture shifting from defending legacy products to reallocating capital.
  • Talent density and meritocracy: Heavy turnover in the C-suite. The new leaders bring payments and scale-SaaS experience. The restructuring delayed into early 2027 suggests layers are being removed, which should help decision speed.
  • Decentralization: Go-to-market is moving toward partner-led sales (Google, Salesforce) and a separate team for standalone AI, which looks like a sensible split into business units.

Ethics

We found no material governance controversies. Executive pay is heavily equity-based, and large RSU grants were made in February 2026. The 2021 Zoom deal was rejected by shareholders, so this board has a history of shareholders pushing back on strategic decisions. That could matter if a take-private bid arrives at a low premium.

Revenue streams, explained simply

  • Core CCaaS subscription (~$223M in Q3, +8% YoY): customers pay per agent seat per month for the cloud contact-center platform. Steady, but growth matches seat growth.
  • AI subscription and usage (~$45M in Q3, +91% YoY): Agent Assist, Genius Routing, IVA and Voice AI Agents, intelligent QA. Usually priced per interaction or per minute. Now 17% of subscription revenue, up from 10% a year ago.
  • Telecom usage (~11% of revenue, ~$36M): pass-through minutes and carrier fees. Low margin, tracks call volume.
  • Professional services (~6%, ~$19M): implementation and AI deployment work. Usually breaks even, but it is the entry point for large migrations.

Nine-month OCF of $164.4M is up 15% YoY.

FCF per share: since the diluted share count is roughly flat (~76M), FCF per share tracks FCF. On an OCF − PP&E capex basis it rose from about $1.06 (FY24: $78.6M) to about $2.63 (FY25: $201.2M). Operating leverage, cost scaling & the flywheel

Operating leverage is visible in GAAP results. GAAP operating income went from −$98.6M in FY23 to −$51.3M in FY24 to +$32.6M in FY25, and reached $34.8M over the first nine months of FY26. Over FY23–FY25, revenue grew 26% while R&D stayed nearly flat (about $160M a year). Adjusted EBITDA margin rose from about 19% (Q1'25) to about 25% (Q3'26). Adjusted gross margin slipped to 61.7% from 62.8%, because of cost timing with a large partner and the data-center refresh. Management expects it to improve in Q4.

Margin expansion drivers: (1) mix shift toward AI and subscription (higher margin than telecom and services); (2) the end of the data-center refresh (capex returns to about 2% of revenue); (3) the 2027 org redesign; (4) Level AI becoming accretive by 2028. Headwinds: GPU and LLM inference costs inside AI products, and partner revenue share on Google-sourced deals.

FlywheelMore interactions → more labeled outcome data → better AI routing and automation → better customer results (lower handle time, higher containment) → more AI consumption and wider deployment (DBRR ↑) → more interactions. Level AI widens the top of this loop to contact centers that don't run Five9.

Risks & Anti-Thesis (Steelman the Bear Case)

▾

If we were short FIVN, these are the five arguments we would make.

1 · AI eats the seat base faster than AI revenue replaces it

Probability: 35%Impact: SeverePartly priced in

Core CCaaS grew only 8%, roughly in line with concurrent seats. If AI agents handle 30–50% of contacts by 2029, seat counts could fall by double digits. AI revenue of about $45M a quarter is still small next to about $223M of seat-based CCaaS. A 10% drop in seats removes about $90M of annual revenue, which is more than the whole AI business generates today. The 91% AI growth also flatters the picture because it starts from a low base. Management itself warned about "difficult year-over-year comparisons for 2027."

2 · Level AI is an expensive admission that internal R&D fell behind

Probability: 40%Impact: ModerateLargely priced in

$325M is 15% of market cap and about 47% of cash, spent on ~$20M of ARR (~16× trailing, ~11× NTM), while Five9 itself trades at 1.7× sales. The deal dilutes margins by about 1 pt in 2027, and management says it is "not counting on measurable financial contribution in the very near term." Five9's past AI acquisitions (Inference Solutions, Aceyus) did not change its growth path. Buying back $325M of stock at 8× earnings could have created more value with certainty.

3 · Competitive squeeze from above and below

Probability: 45%Impact: HighPartly priced in

NICE (2.4× Five9's revenue, $392M AI ARR) and Genesys own the top of the enterprise market. Piper Sandler noted in January 2026 that Five9 is "less competitive at the high end." Hyperscalers (Google CES, AWS Connect, Microsoft Dynamics Contact Center) can bundle AI agents almost for free. AI-native players (Sierra, Decagon) sell straight to CX leaders and skip CCaaS entirely. Google is a partner today and a possible competitor tomorrow. Over 80% partner-influenced revenue means Five9 relies on companies whose incentives may change.

4 · Execution risk from a rebuilt leadership team

Probability: 30%Impact: ModerateNot fully priced in

The CEO (since Feb 2026), CFO, CTO, CSO, CMO, and EVP of Strategy have all been in their roles less than about 18 months. The org redesign slipped from Q4'26 to early 2027, the Investor Day slipped to 2027, and FY EPS was cut despite a revenue beat. Insiders sold steadily from $18 to $35 with no open-market buys. A restructuring during sales-force integration (adding the Level AI team) risks disrupting the pipeline just as tough AI comps arrive.

5 · Balance sheet and macro sensitivity

Probability: 20%Impact: ModerateNot priced in

After Level AI closes, net debt is about $370M against $747.5M of 1% converts due 2029. Refinancing in 2028–29 at 5–7% would cost $30–45M a year in extra interest, about 15–20% of FCF. Contact-center volume and seats are cyclical: a recession hits BPOs and retail customers first, and the stock's 1.55 beta reflects that. Telecom usage (11% of revenue) swings with call volume.

Our rebuttal, in briefRisk #1 is the main debate, but the data so far argues against it. Subscription retention rose from 105% to 109% while AI adoption sped up, which means existing customers are spending more, not less. Risk #2 is real, but at $325M it is a bounded mistake, not a fatal one. Risks #3–5 are why the stock sits at 7× EBITDA rather than 15×.

Relative valuation read-through

Among direct CCaaS peers, Five9 has the fastest growth and the second-lowest multiple. NICE trades cheaper on EV/EBITDA (5.5×), but its cloud growth has been cut twice this year (to about 12% from 14.5–15%), its AI ARR growth is slowing (52% → 46%), and FCF fell to $69M from $164M. NICE's momentum is fading while Five9's is building. RingCentral grows less than half as fast at about 6% but trades at a higher P/S (2.4×) and a forward P/E near 15×. Twilio shows what a re-rating looks like: once growth reached about 20% with strong FCF, the market paid about 6× sales.

If Five9 simply traded at RingCentral's ~2.4× sales, the stock would be about $40. At 10× EV/EBITDA, in line with RNG, it would be about $38. Neither scenario requires Five9 to be valued as an AI winner. Both only require the market to stop pricing it as a loser.

First-Principles KPIs

▾

These five leading indicators will settle the AI-casualty vs. AI-beneficiary debate before the income statement does:

  • KPI 1 · AI revenue as a % of subscription: 10% → 17% in a year. The thesis needs about 25% by end-2027. This is the clearest single measure of whether outcome-based monetization is replacing seats.
  • KPI 2 · LTM subscription DBRR: 105/107% → 109%. Above 110% means AI expansion is outrunning seat losses. A drop below 105% would break the thesis.
  • KPI 3 · Subscription growth minus core CCaaS growth (the "AI spread"): Q3: 16% − 8% = 8 pts. A widening spread means AI is carrying the growth.
  • KPI 4 · Adj. EBITDA margin net of inference costs: 22.4–25.7% range. It needs to hold at 24% or higher while absorbing Level AI, which would show AI is not lowering unit economics.
  • KPI 5 · Concurrent seat growth: currently about equal to CCaaS growth (~8%). A turn negative would confirm the bear case's seat-cannibalization fear. Watch disclosures closely.

Glossary & Methodology

▾
↩ back

P/S (Price-to-Sales)

Market Cap ÷ Trailing-12-Month Revenue

Shows how much investors pay per dollar of revenue. It is useful for companies whose GAAP earnings are held down by non-cash charges. FTA scale: >30 Euphoric, >18 Excited, >8 Optimistic, 4–8 Aware, <4 Cautious, <2 Pessimistic.

↩ back

P/E (Price-to-Earnings)

Share Price ÷ EPS

Measures how many years of current earnings you are paying for. "Forward" uses expected next-year EPS. GAAP and non-GAAP P/E can differ a lot for software companies.

↩ back

EV/EBITDA

(Market Cap + Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA

A valuation that accounts for both debt and cash. It makes companies with different balance sheets comparable. Below 8× is usually considered value territory for software.

↩ back

Adjusted EBITDA

Net Income + Interest + Taxes + D&A + Stock Comp + One-offs

A management-defined profit measure that strips out non-cash and non-recurring items. It is useful, but it flatters companies that pay heavily in stock, so always check it against FCF.

↩ back

Non-GAAP EPS

Adjusted Net Income ÷ Diluted Shares

Earnings per share that exclude stock comp, amortization of acquired intangibles, and similar items. This is the figure Wall Street consensus tracks.

↩ back

DBRR / DBNRR (Dollar-Based Retention Rate)

LTM revenue from a customer cohort ÷ that cohort's revenue a year earlier

Above 100% means existing customers spend more over time, net of churn and downgrades. It is the cleanest read on whether customers are expanding or shrinking.

↩ back

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)

Current monthly recurring revenue × 12

The annualized value of recurring contracts, a forward-looking snapshot of subscription scale.

↩ back

TCV (Total Contract Value)

Sum of all payments over the full contract term

Shows the size of a multi-year deal. A $100M TCV contract over five years is about $20M a year.

↩ back

RPO (Remaining Performance Obligations)

Contracted revenue not yet recognized

Backlog of committed revenue. It can jump when one customer signs a longer contract, so management says it is not a forecasting tool.

↩ back

ASR (Accelerated Share Repurchase)

Company pays a bank upfront → receives most shares immediately

A way to retire a large block of stock quickly. The final price is settled later against the average price over the period.

↩ back

TAM (Total Addressable Market)

The total revenue opportunity if a company captured 100% of its market. For AI-for-CX companies, the relevant TAM can include part of the labor budget, not only software spend.

↩ back

CCaaS (Contact Center as a Service)

Cloud-hosted contact-center software (routing, IVR, recording, analytics, AI) sold by subscription, as opposed to on-premise hardware such as legacy Avaya or Cisco systems.

↩ back

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

(End ÷ Start)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1

The smoothed annual growth rate over a period, which removes year-to-year noise.

↩ back

FCF Yield

Free Cash Flow ÷ Market Cap

The cash return the business generates relative to its price. Above 8% for a growing company is unusual and often signals mispricing or hidden risk.

↩ back

Moat

A durable competitive advantage (switching costs, network effects, scale, data, brand) that protects returns from competition over time.

↩ back

Current Ratio

Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Short-term liquidity. Above 1.5× is comfortable. Five9 is at about 4.0×.

↩ back

Operating Leverage

% change in operating income ÷ % change in revenue

When costs grow more slowly than revenue, each extra dollar of sales adds more profit. This is the main way software margins expand.

↩ back

Probability-Weighted Price Target

Σ (Scenario Price × Probability)

The expected value across bull, base, and bear outcomes, which forces you to think about the full distribution rather than a single point.
