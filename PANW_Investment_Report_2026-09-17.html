Executive Summary & Investment Thesis

▾
Thesis (Bear on price, Bull on franchise): Palo Alto Networks is the single best-positioned public company for the world McKinsey's Technology Trends Outlook 2026 describes — one where more than three-quarters of exploited vulnerabilities are zero-day, exploitation of public-facing applications is up 44% in a year, and the only defense that works is a unified platform operating at machine speed. Fiscal 2026 proved it: nearly $1 billion of net new NGS ARR in a single quarter, a record ~220 net new platformizations, RPO above $21 billion, and Prisma AIRS reaching $100 million ARR faster than any product in company history. But the market has already paid for that future. At $375 the stock trades at 26.7x trailing sales, ~90x forward non-GAAP earnings and ~57x forward adjusted free cash flow, while the FY27 guide implies NGS ARR growth decelerating from 63% (acquisition-inflated) to 22–23%, with organic net new ARR roughly flat year over year. Our probability-weighted 1-year target is $360 (−4%); our 3-year target is $434 (+16%, ~5% CAGR). We would become buyers below ~$300, where the 3-year base case compounds at a double-digit rate.

Palo Alto reported Q4 FY26 (quarter ended July 31, 2026) on September 1. Revenue grew 34% to $3.41B against a $3.345–3.355B guide; NGS ARR reached $9.10B (guide $8.90–8.95B); non-GAAP EPS was $1.02 versus $0.96–0.98; adjusted FCF was $1.29B (+35%). Full-year revenue was $11.48B (+24%), adjusted FCF $4.41B (38.4% margin), non-GAAP operating margin 29.2% (+40 bps despite absorbing two lower-margin acquisitions). Management guided FY27 revenue to $14.10–14.20B (+23–24%), NGS ARR to $11.075–11.175B (+22–23%), 29.5% non-GAAP operating margin, $4.16–4.19 EPS and a 38% adjusted FCF margin. The stock, which had run from a March low near $140 to an all-time high of $398.88 in August, fell after the print and has since consolidated around $375.

Why is the stock up 86% in a year? Three things happened at once. First, the "Mythos moment": cyber-capable frontier models (Anthropic's Mythos, Project Glasswing) demonstrated that AI can find and exploit enterprise technical debt in minutes, moving real-time defense from roadmap to requirement and driving two consecutive quarters of accelerating bookings. Second, Palo Alto closed the two largest deals in its history — CyberArk (~$25B, now "Idira", closed Feb 11, 2026) and Chronosphere ($3.35B, closed Jan 29, 2026) — and both are scaling faster inside Palo Alto than they did standalone. Third, the market re-rated the whole "platform consolidator" cohort (Fortinet +116%, CrowdStrike +121% over 52 weeks) as McKinsey and others made concentration toward a few dominant platforms the consensus view.

What the market is missing

  • The 63% NGS ARR growth is two-thirds inorganic. Of the $3.52B of net new NGS ARR added in FY26, roughly $1.6B arrived with CyberArk and Chronosphere. Organic NGS ARR growth was in the high-20s, and the FY27 guide implies ~$2.0B of net new ARR — essentially flat against FY26's organic addition, with 60–61% of it back-half weighted and a nine-figure one-time LLM-customer migration to Chronosphere still washing through Q1.
  • The FY27 revenue guide is mostly a full-year of acquired revenue. Idira contributed $644M of reported revenue in FY26 (five and a half months); FY27 guidance assumes ~$1.5B. Network & AI Security — 73% of revenue — is guided to "low double digits." Strip out M&A and Palo Alto is a mid-teens organic grower priced at 21.6x forward sales.
  • GAAP earnings have gone to zero, and the gap is structural. FY26 GAAP net income was $307M on $11.5B of revenue (2.7% margin) because SBC ($1.71B), acquired-intangible amortization ($638M, now running ~$1.1B annualized) and a $562M non-cash convertible fair-value charge sit between non-GAAP and GAAP. Share count grew 7.8% YoY (mostly CyberArk stock consideration) and the $1.0B remaining buyback authorization — expiring December 31, 2026 — was not used.
  • The CEO bought at $147, not $375. Nikesh Arora's discretionary $10M open-market purchase on March 27, 2026 was a strong signal at that price. He has sold an estimated $1.1B of stock since 2021. The signal at today's price is silence.

At 26.7x trailing sales PANW sits in the "Excited" band of our P/S scale (18–30x), one notch below Euphoric, for a business whose organic revenue growth is mid-teens and whose guided total growth is 23–24%. The McKinsey thesis is right about the industry — the defense window has collapsed and value will concentrate in a handful of platforms — but "right about the industry" and "right about the price" are different claims. Our verdict is a tactical bear for the next twelve months, with an explicit re-entry zone below ~$300. Microeconomic & Valuation Analysis

▾

Industry & TAM

Palo Alto now sells three platforms: Network & AI Security (hardware and software firewalls, Prisma SASE, cloud-delivered security services, Prisma AIRS for AI runtime security — $8.35B FY26 revenue, +17%), Cortex (XSIAM security operations, cloud security, Chronosphere observability — $1.92B, +25%) and Idira (the former CyberArk identity and privileged-access business — $1.26B pro forma, +21%). Management sizes the combined TAM at $340B by 2030; third-party estimates for the segments Palo Alto actually competes in (network security, SASE, SIEM/SOC, cloud security, identity, AI security) sum to roughly $120–150B growing low-to-mid teens. The company's $11.5B of revenue is therefore high-single-digit penetration of the narrow market and low-single-digit penetration of the broad one.

The structural driver is what McKinsey calls the collapse of the defense window. When more than 75% of exploited vulnerabilities are zero-day and cyber-capable models can chain flaws in minutes, the economics of a "best-of-breed" stack of 30 tools with human-speed correlation stop working. The buyer wants one data lake, one policy engine and machine-speed remediation — which is why McKinsey's prescription (continuous runtime security, automated containment, agentic security operations, identity, post-quantum migration) reads like a Palo Alto product catalog. Management's own framing is that there is roughly $1 trillion of global "cybersecurity debt" that must be modernized on unified platforms. The important nuance for investors is that this thesis is now consensus, and the report itself flags the corollary: value accrues to a few platforms, and those platforms are already priced as winners.

GAAP earnings multiples are not useful here — not because Palo Alto is unprofitable in cash terms (it converts 38% of revenue to adjusted FCF), but because $1.7B of stock-based compensation and a now-large acquired-intangible amortization charge sit between the two. The right lenses are price-to-free-cash-flow and EV/Sales relative to growth. On those, PANW is expensive by any historical standard for itself: it spent most of 2023–2025 between 12x and 17x sales and 35–50x FCF. Today's 26.7x / ~70x is a re-rating, not an earnings story — roughly 60% of the 52-week return came from multiple expansion. A Rule-of-40 score of ~62 (24% revenue growth + 38% FCF margin) is genuinely elite and justifies a premium; the question is whether it justifies a premium to CrowdStrike on a growth-adjusted basis, which it now carries (see Section 9).

Institutional ownership & insider activity

Institutions own about 83.6% of shares outstanding; insiders own 0.72%. Short interest is 21.4M shares (2.6% of float, 3.8 days to cover) and fell month-over-month from 22.4M — there is no crowded short to squeeze. Share count rose 7.8% YoY to 818M, the bulk of it the ~110M shares issued as CyberArk consideration (2.2005 PANW shares plus $45 cash per CyberArk share).

Insider activity cuts both ways. CEO Nikesh Arora has been a persistent seller since 2021 (secondary sources estimate ~4.9M shares and ~$1.1B of proceeds, much of it under 10b5-1 plans), yet on March 27, 2026 he made a discretionary open-market purchase of 68,085 shares at ~$146.88 (~$10M) — rare for a mega-cap CEO and, in hindsight, near the exact bottom of the software selloff. In June 2026 he reallocated 865,090 phantom shares within a deferred-compensation plan (no open-market effect). We read the March purchase as management believing $147 was cheap; we have no evidence management believes $375 is.

Analyst growth estimates

Consensus (55 analysts) rates PANW "Buy" with an average price target of $393 — 5% above the current price, the smallest implied upside in the peer group after CrowdStrike (−5%) and Fortinet (−5%). Roughly 89% of ratings are Buy or Strong Buy. The three-year consensus revenue growth forecast is ~17% CAGR and EPS growth ~14%, which yields a PEG near 4.8x. Management's FY30 framework — $20B of NGS ARR and 4,000+ platformizations — implies NGS ARR compounding ~22% from FY26, in line with the FY27 guide; we model total revenue of ~$22B in FY30 in the base case.

Qualitative Analysis — Moat, Management & Culture

▾

Competitive moat — a systems view

Palo Alto's moat is not any single product; several of its products face a better point-solution competitor (CrowdStrike in endpoint, Zscaler in zero-trust access, Okta in workforce identity, Datadog in observability). The moat is the system: the largest installed base of enforcement points in enterprise security (70,000+ customers, firewalls that inspect and block 30 billion attacks a day), a single telemetry lake (XSIAM) that every module feeds, and a commercial model — platformization — that gives customers financial incentives (staggered payments, early deployment, "free" migration periods) to standardize. Four reinforcing consequences:

  • Data gravity at machine speed. Real-time defense requires that the firewall, the SASE fabric, the endpoint agent, the identity vault and the SOC all share one policy plane. Once a customer's telemetry lives in XSIAM (now 1,000+ customers, $700M+ ARR, +70%), adding cloud security or exposure management is "querying existing data in new ways," not an integration project. Mean time to respond falls from days to under 10 minutes.
  • Cross-sell arithmetic. Platformized customers show net revenue retention above 120%; $5M+ deals in Idira grew 50% YoY two quarters after close; XSIAM contributed 50% of net new cross-sell logos for observability. The Q4 $72M deal — eight-figure commitments across all three platforms from one IT services firm — is the model working.
  • Incumbent displacement. SASE bookings grew 40% in FY26 with legacy displacements in ~100 accounts worth $400M+ TCV, nearly double the prior year, moving Palo Alto to the #2 SASE position. Because the customer already runs Strata Cloud Manager and the GlobalProtect agent, swapping the SASE layer is a small change rather than a rip-and-replace.
  • M&A as a product strategy. Arora is explicit that acquisitions are "a consequence of product strategy": the company tracks 40–50 funded startups and buys the one that "has the strategy right" when a market moves faster than internal R&D can. The FY26 tuck-ins — Koi ($231M, agentic endpoint), Portkey ($117M, AI gateway now GA inside Prisma AIRS), Embrace (real-user monitoring), Console (agentic IT/security ops) — are precisely the runtime-security pieces McKinsey highlights.

Competitive set: CrowdStrike (endpoint-led platform, Falcon Flex, the only other consolidator with comparable scale), Fortinet (price-competitive firewall incumbent with the best margins in the group), Zscaler (SASE/zero-trust leader now being displaced by Palo Alto in large accounts), Microsoft (bundled Defender/Sentinel/Entra — the most dangerous long-run competitor because it is "free"), Cloudflare (network-edge and post-quantum angle), plus hyperscaler-native security and a long tail of AI-security startups.

Founder-led? No — but operator-led in the best sense.

Founder Nir Zuk (CTO) departed in 2025; Nikesh Arora (ex-SoftBank president, ex-Google chief business officer) has been chairman and CEO since June 2018. Under Arora the company went from a single-product firewall vendor with roughly $2.3B of revenue to an $11.5B three-platform company, NGS ARR went from under $1B to $9.1B, non-GAAP operating margin expanded more than 500 bps in the last three years alone, and the split-adjusted share price has compounded at roughly 35% per year. He has executed 20-plus acquisitions with a consistent playbook — buy the category leader early, fold it into the platform, expand margins — and the CyberArk integration (synergy targets 3–6 months ahead of plan, 1,000+ bps of margin improvement in two quarters) is the largest test of that playbook yet. CFO Dipak Golechha (since 2021) has delivered an adjusted FCF margin of 38%+ in each of the last four years while the business shifted from multi-year to annual billing (6% of bookings in FY20 → ~30% today), a transition that would have cratered cash flow at a less disciplined company.

Capital allocation

The record is strong on M&A returns and weak on per-share discipline. The company deploys cash almost exclusively into acquisitions (CyberArk, Chronosphere, IBM QRadar SaaS, Talon, Dig, Protect AI, Koi, Portkey) and has let the $1.0B remaining repurchase authorization sit unused into its December 2026 expiry while share count grew 7.8%. Goodwill is now $22.0B of $48.5B total assets. Given ~$7.9B of cash and investments, ~$4.4B of annual adjusted FCF and only $1.77B of (assumed CyberArk) convertible debt, the balance sheet could support meaningful buybacks; the choice not to is a statement that management sees better returns in deals — and, implicitly, that it does not see its own stock as cheap.

Culture & organizational traits

  • Customer obsession: Platformization is structured around the customer's consolidation economics rather than Palo Alto's quarter; management openly finances migrations. NRR above 120% in the platformized cohort is the evidence.
  • Innovation pace: FY26 GAAP R&D was $2.55B (22% of revenue). Prisma AIRS went from GA to $100M ARR in four quarters with 800+ customers; the company is the first certified commercial partner for Mythos-class models through its Frontier AI Defense service; Unit 42 can build and deploy signatures for new OT/open-source vulnerabilities in under four hours.
  • Failure tolerance / idea meritocracy: The 2024 decision to give away product to accelerate platformization — which cut near-term billings guidance and cost the stock 28% in a day — was a bet on long-term architecture over short-term optics. Two years later it is the growth engine. That is a management team willing to be wrong in public.
  • Talent density: 21,921 employees generating ~$524K revenue per head (up from ~$470K pre-CyberArk despite absorbing 3,000+ lower-productivity acquired staff); the company cut 500+ CyberArk roles post-close to hit synergy targets.
  • Ethics / governance watch-items: Persistent CEO selling, an unused buyback, aggressive non-GAAP adjustments (acquisition costs, convertible fair-value swings, a large negative "tax adjustment"), and a 42.7% GAAP effective tax rate distorted by the CyberArk structure. None disqualifying; all worth watching.

Revenue streams, explained simply

Palo Alto reports two lines. Product revenue (20% of FY26, $2.28B, +27%) is mostly hardware firewalls recognized when shipped — the Gen 5 appliance cycle and AI data-center build-outs drove an unusually strong year, but management says hardware is ~10% of total revenue and faces rising memory/storage costs. Subscription and support revenue (80%, $9.20B, +24%) is recognized ratably and includes cloud-delivered security services attached to firewalls, SASE, Cortex, Idira and legacy support contracts. Beginning this quarter the company also discloses revenue by platform: Network & AI Security $8.35B (+17%), Cortex $1.92B (+25%), Idira $644M reported / $1.26B pro forma (+21%), plus professional services and other. NGS ARR — the metric that matters — strips out hardware and legacy attached subscriptions/support and is now 79% of the revenue run-rate.

FY26 operating cash flow was $4.55B (+23%), capex $440M (including a $91M land purchase), free cash flow $4.11B and company-defined adjusted FCF $4.41B (38.4% margin) after adding back $164M of acquisition-related payments. FCF per share (GAAP definition) is ~$5.03 on 818M shares, up from ~$5.20 on 669M shares a year earlier — per-share free cash flow fell ~3% despite 19% aggregate growth, because the CyberArk shares arrived before CyberArk's cash flow did. That inverts in FY27 as Idira contributes a full year at improving margins: at the guided 38% margin on $14.15B, FY27 adjusted FCF is ~$5.4B, or ~$6.35 per share on the guided 845M diluted count (+26%).

Two cash-flow nuances matter. First, the shift to annual billing (now ~30% of bookings, up from 6% in FY20) has depressed reported FCF relative to bookings for five years; management says the transition has "largely stabilized," which — if true — removes a headwind and makes the 40% FY28 target credible. Second, deferred revenue of $14.76B (current $7.75B, long-term $7.01B) and RPO of $21.2B give unusually long revenue visibility: current RPO alone covers ~66% of FY27 guided revenue.

Non-GAAP operating leverage is real: FY26 non-GAAP operating income grew 27% on 24% revenue growth even while consolidating two businesses that ran at much lower standalone margins, and management targets a further 30 bps in FY27 with synergies "more than offsetting" higher cloud-hosting and hardware-component costs. Gross margin is also under mild, structural pressure: the fastest-growing offerings are SaaS products that have not reached margin maturity, and management expects cloud-hosting costs to grow faster than revenue in FY27.

The flywheel

Firewall installed base → attach cloud-delivered subscriptions → migrate to SASE on the same management plane → route telemetry into XSIAM → cross-sell cloud security, exposure management, observability → add identity (Idira) and AI runtime (Prisma AIRS) as the agentic surface expands → NRR >120% on platformized customers → larger, longer contracts (RPO +34%) → 38%+ FCF funds the next category acquisition → repeat. The FY26 data shows every stage turning; the FY27 guide shows the flywheel's organic torque is steady rather than accelerating.

The current ratio of 0.87x looks weak but is a feature of the model: $7.75B of the $9.92B in current liabilities is deferred revenue — cash already collected for service not yet delivered. Liquidity is ample at ~$7.9B of cash and investments against $1.77B of zero-coupon convertible notes assumed from CyberArk (fair-value swings on those notes and their capped calls produced a $524M non-cash charge in Q4 alone). The real balance-sheet story is goodwill: $22.0B of it, up from $4.6B a year ago, plus $7.0B of intangibles. That is the price of buying CyberArk at ~20x revenue, and it will be tested by impairment review every year. Dilution is the other watch-item: 7.8% share growth in one year, SBC running ~$1.7–1.9B annually, and a buyback authorization that expires unused on December 31, 2026 unless renewed.

Growth rates vs. industry

Revenue CAGR FY22→FY26 was ~20% (from $5.50B to $11.48B), against a security software market growing 12–14%; NGS ARR compounded ~48% over the same window with acquisitions. Organic revenue growth was 14–16% for eight consecutive quarters before the deals closed. The FY27 guide (+23–24% total, low-double-digit for the largest platform) implies organic growth in the mid-teens — roughly 1.2x the market rate — which is good, durable, and not what a 21.6x forward sales multiple usually buys.

Risks & Anti-Thesis — Steelmanning the Other Side

▾

Because our verdict is a valuation bear, the "anti-thesis" here runs in both directions: the first three risks are what could make the stock fall harder than our base case; the last two are the strongest arguments against our bear call — the ways we could be wrong by being too cautious.

1. The FY27 guide implies organic deceleration, and the Street is modeling acceleration

Probability 45%Impact HighPriced in No

FY26 net new NGS ARR was $3.52B, of which ~$1.6B was acquired and a nine-figure slice was a one-time Chronosphere migration; organic net new ARR was therefore roughly $1.8–1.9B. The FY27 guide midpoint ($11.125B) implies ~$2.0B of net new ARR — flat to slightly up — with Network & AI Security (73% of revenue) guided to "low double digits." If the AI-panic bookings surge of Q3–Q4 FY26 was a pull-forward rather than a new run-rate, FY28 growth lands in the mid-teens and the stock's 21.6x forward sales multiple has no growth to support it. A de-rating to 14–15x forward sales (its own 2024–25 range) is a ~$250–275 stock.

Our rebuttal: Palo Alto has guided conservatively for eight straight quarters, RPO is growing faster than revenue (34% vs 24%), and 60% of net new ARR is back-half weighted — a typical Arora "floor" setup. But the burden of proof is on the bulls: this is the risk that defines the next two prints.

2. GAAP earnings are near zero and the non-GAAP bridge is getting wider, not narrower

Probability 70%Impact MediumPriced in Partially

FY26 GAAP net income was $307M — 2.7% of revenue — versus $2.93B non-GAAP. The bridge is $1.71B of SBC, $638M of intangible amortization (annualizing at ~$1.1B post-CyberArk), $295M of acquisition costs and a $562M non-cash convertible fair-value charge. Share count grew 7.8%. On an "owner earnings" basis (adjusted FCF minus SBC minus cash acquisition costs) the company generated ~$2.5B, putting the stock at ~120x. If rates stay high and the market rotates back toward GAAP profitability — as it did in early 2026, when PANW fell to $140 — the same fundamentals could carry a much lower multiple.

Our rebuttal: SBC as a percentage of revenue has been stable at ~15% for three years and amortization is genuinely non-cash. But a $1.0B buyback authorization left unused into expiry, at a time when the company issued 110M shares for a deal, is a capital-allocation signal we weight in the conviction score.

3. CyberArk goodwill, integration fatigue and the "acquisition treadmill"

Probability 30%Impact HighPriced in No

Goodwill and intangibles are $29B — 60% of assets — and CyberArk was bought at roughly 20x revenue. Identity is a market where Okta, Microsoft Entra and SailPoint compete hard, and where the "agentic identity" TAM is a thesis rather than a revenue line. Idira is guided to high-teens to 20% pro forma growth; if it slips into the low teens (as many acquired security assets do once the founding team departs — 500+ CyberArk roles were cut post-close), the company faces an impairment charge, a credibility hit to the M&A playbook, and the temptation to buy the next thing to restore headline growth. Arora explicitly monitors "40–50 funded startups"; the treadmill is the strategy.

Our rebuttal: Two quarters of data show Idira accelerating inside Palo Alto (bookings growing faster than revenue, $5M+ deals +50%, synergies 3–6 months ahead of plan). The track record with Demisto, Twistlock, Expanse, Talon and Dig is strong. But this is the largest deal by 5x, and the base rate for $25B software acquisitions is not kind.

4. Anti-bear #1: The Mythos moment is a regime change, not a cycle — and platform concentration means PANW takes a disproportionate share

Probability 40%Impact HighPriced in Partially

McKinsey's central claim is that the defense window has collapsed permanently: attackers with cyber-capable models exploit technical debt in minutes, and the only viable response is a unified, machine-speed platform. If that is right, the $1 trillion of "cybersecurity debt" Arora describes gets modernized on two or three platforms over five years, and the current 22–23% guide is the floor of a multi-year re-acceleration. Bookings accelerated for two consecutive quarters; agentic traffic on Prisma SASE rose 9x in nine months; Prisma AIRS added $100M of ARR in four quarters with 800+ customers; observability doubled to $500M+ in two quarters. In that world PANW compounds revenue at 20%+ through FY30 and a 25x sales multiple is defensible — which is our bull case ($470 in a year, $587 in three).

Why we still lean bear: we assign this 25–30% probability, not 50%, because the FY27 guide — written by a management team that sees the pipeline — does not yet show it in the numbers. We would rather pay 18x for proof than 22x for a promise.

5. Anti-bear #2: Free-cash-flow compounding plus a real buyback could make today's multiple look cheap

Probability 35%Impact MediumPriced in Partially

At a 40% FCF margin on $22B of FY30 revenue, Palo Alto generates ~$8.8B of free cash a year with ~$8B already on the balance sheet. If the annual-billing transition really has stabilized and the company redirects even half its FCF to repurchases from FY28, share count could shrink 2–3% a year instead of growing 4–5%, adding ~5 points to per-share FCF growth. Fortinet — a slower grower — trades at 40x FCF with a 3.4% buyback yield; a Palo Alto that buys back stock while growing 17% would deserve 50x, and 50x on FY30 FCF is a ~$500 stock in 2029.

Why we still lean bear: the company has shown no inclination to buy back stock at any price in the last two years, and the ~5% probability-weighted 3-year CAGR in our model already assumes a 45x exit multiple. The buyback is upside optionality, not the base case.

Other risks noted
Microsoft bundling Defender/Sentinel/Entra into E5 at effectively zero marginal cost; hyperscaler-native security for single-cloud shops; rising memory and storage costs in the ~10%-of-revenue hardware line; cloud-hosting COGS growing faster than revenue in FY27; concentration of Chronosphere in a handful of AI labs (one customer was a nine-figure ARR event); Israel-based operations and export-control exposure; a product vulnerability in Palo Alto's own firewalls (PAN-OS CVEs have been actively exploited before) is the reputational tail risk for a "platform of trust"; and the December 31, 2026 buyback expiry.

Glossary & Methodology

▾

Methodology

All company financial figures come from Palo Alto Networks' SEC-filed 8-K earnings releases (Q1 FY25 through Q4 FY26), the Q4 FY26 earnings call transcript and the FY26 10-K, with market data from StockAnalysis / S&P Global Market Intelligence as of the September 17, 2026 close. Peer data is from the same source and date. Where a quarterly figure was not stated directly (Q1/Q2 FY25 and Q2 FY26 adjusted FCF; Q1 FY26 NGS ARR; Q1 FY25 EPS guidance), it is derived from disclosed annual totals or growth rates and marked "~". Insider-transaction estimates are from secondary aggregators of Form 4 filings. The McKinsey framing is from the Technology Trends Outlook 2026 as summarized in the FTA investor brief. Scenario targets apply EV/adjusted-FCF and EV/Sales multiples to FTA's own revenue and margin model; probabilities are judgmental. Nothing here is investment advice.

Next-Generation Security ARR (NGS ARR)

Annualized allocated revenue of all active contracts, excluding hardware, legacy attached subscriptions, support and professional services

Palo Alto's headline growth metric, covering SASE, software firewalls, cloud-delivered security services, Cortex, Idira, observability and AI security. Why it matters: it isolates the recurring, platform-driven business from the hardware and legacy support lines — but it includes acquired ARR from the day a deal closes, so headline growth must be read alongside the organic figure.

↑ Back to Section 1

Net New NGS ARR

NGS ARR this quarter − NGS ARR last quarter

Captures new logos and expansion net of churn (and, when relevant, acquired ARR). Why it matters: the level and trend of organic net new ARR is the cleanest real-time read on demand; Q4 FY26's ~$1B included a nine-figure one-time migration.

↑ Back to Section 10

Remaining Performance Obligation (RPO)

Total contracted revenue not yet recognized; current RPO (cRPO) = the portion due within 12 months

A bookings-backlog measure that includes both billed (deferred revenue) and unbilled contracts. Why it matters: RPO growth (34%) running ahead of revenue growth (24%) signals future revenue acceleration or, at minimum, visibility; $21.2B is ~1.5 years of guided revenue.

↑ Back to Section 1

Platformization

A customer standardizing on one or more Palo Alto platforms (Network & AI Security, Cortex, Idira), typically after consolidating point products from other vendors

Palo Alto counts "net new platformizations" among its ~5,000 largest customers and targets 4,000+ by FY30. Why it matters: platformized customers carry NRR above 120% and larger, longer contracts; the count is the leading indicator for the consolidation thesis.

↑ Back to Section 1

Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

NRR = ARR today from customers who existed 12 months ago ÷ their ARR 12 months ago

Above 100% means the existing base grows through upsell net of churn. Palo Alto discloses it only for the platformized cohort (>120%). Why it matters: at 120%, the platformized base grows ~20% with zero new customers.

↑ Back to Section 3

Adjusted Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Adj. FCF = Operating cash flow − capex + HQ/corporate-asset capex + acquisition-related payments + litigation payments

Palo Alto's company-defined cash metric; unadjusted FCF (OCF − capex) was $4.11B in FY26 vs $4.41B adjusted. FCF margin = FCF ÷ revenue. Why it matters: for a company with near-zero GAAP earnings, FCF is the only cash profitability measure — but it flatters companies with high stock-based compensation (see SBC) and, in Palo Alto's case, adds back real cash spent on deals.

↑ Back to Section 1

Stock-Based Compensation (SBC)

Non-cash expense for equity granted to employees; added back in FCF and non-GAAP earnings

A real cost borne by shareholders through dilution rather than cash. Why it matters: Palo Alto's $1.71B of FY26 SBC (14.9% of revenue) is the largest single item between its 29% non-GAAP operating margin and its 6% GAAP operating margin.

↑ Back to Section 2

Pro Forma Growth

Growth calculated as if an acquired business had been owned for the full prior-year comparison period

Used by Palo Alto for Idira ($1.26B pro forma FY26 revenue, +21%) and for "organic bookings acceleration." Why it matters: reported growth (+34% in Q4) overstates the underlying business when large deals close mid-year; pro forma growth is closer to the truth investors are paying for.

↑ Back to Section 4

Price-to-Sales (P/S) and the FTA Scale

P/S = Market cap ÷ TTM revenue · Scale: >30 Euphoric · >18 Excited · >8 Optimistic · 4–8 Aware · <4 Cautious · <2 Pessimistic

Useful for companies where GAAP P/E fails. Why it matters: PANW at 26.7x is "Excited" — a multiple that requires sustained 20%+ growth and margin expansion to earn; its own 2024–25 range was 12–17x ("Optimistic").

↑ Back to Section 2

Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow (P/FCF)

P/FCF = Market cap ÷ TTM free cash flow (EV/FCF uses enterprise value)

The cash-flow analogue of P/E. Why it matters: PANW at ~70x adjusted FCF (57x forward) is priced for many years of 20%+ cash-flow growth; Fortinet, the mature comp, trades at 40x.

↑ Back to Section 2

EV / EBITDA

Enterprise value ÷ earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation & amortization

Capital-structure-neutral profitability multiple. Why it matters: at 196x on GAAP EBITDA it is not meaningful for PANW today because SBC is not added back under GAAP; on a non-GAAP basis (~$3.9B EBITDA) it is ~78x.

↑ Back to Section 9

Rule of 40

Revenue growth % + FCF margin % ≥ 40

A SaaS heuristic for balancing growth and profitability. Why it matters: PANW scores ~62 on reported growth (24 + 38) and ~53 on organic growth, both elite; management calls itself a "Rule of 50" company.

↑ Back to Section 2

Operating Leverage

% change in operating income ÷ % change in revenue

When costs grow slower than revenue, margins expand. Why it matters: PANW's non-GAAP operating income grew 27% on 24% revenue growth in FY26 while absorbing lower-margin acquisitions — positive leverage; GAAP operating income fell 44% — negative leverage, driven by deal accounting.

↑ Back to Section 4

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

CAGR = (End ÷ Start)^(1/years) − 1

Smoothed annual growth across multi-year periods. Why it matters: FY22–FY26 revenue CAGR of ~20% vs ~13% for the security-software market; our 3-year base case implies only ~5% share-price CAGR because of multiple compression.

↑ Back to Section 2

Share Dilution

YoY % change in shares outstanding

New shares from SBC, option exercises, convertibles or stock-funded acquisitions reduce each existing share's claim. Why it matters: at +7.8% in FY26 (mostly the ~110M CyberArk shares), PANW's per-share FCF fell even as total FCF grew 26%.

↑ Back to Section 4

Current Ratio / Working Capital

Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities · Working capital = the difference

Short-term liquidity. Why it matters: PANW's 0.87x looks weak but $7.75B of current liabilities is deferred revenue (cash already collected), not debt; adjusted for that, liquidity is very strong.

↑ Back to Section 4

Goodwill & Acquired Intangibles

Goodwill = purchase price − fair value of identifiable net assets; intangibles = acquired technology, customer relationships, trade names (amortized)

PANW carries $22.0B of goodwill and $7.0B of intangibles after CyberArk and Chronosphere. Why it matters: intangible amortization (~$1.1B/yr) depresses GAAP earnings and gross margin; goodwill is tested annually and an impairment would signal the deal underperformed.

↑ Back to Section 4

Convertible Senior Notes

Debt that can convert into equity at a preset price; here CyberArk's 0% notes due 2030, carried at fair value after the acquisition

Assumed in the CyberArk deal and marked to market each quarter with offsetting capped calls. Why it matters: the fair-value swing ($524M in Q4) is non-cash and excluded from non-GAAP EPS, but it is why GAAP net income turned negative; the notes are a 2030 refinancing question.

↑ Back to Section 4

Short Interest

Shares sold short ÷ float (or shares outstanding); days to cover = short shares ÷ average daily volume

Measures bearish positioning. Why it matters: at 2.6% of float and falling, there is no crowded short in PANW — a valuation bear call here cannot rely on a squeeze-driven bounce to be wrong quickly, nor on forced covering to be right.

TAM (Total Addressable Market)

Annual revenue opportunity if a company captured 100% of its target market

Vendor-stated TAMs (PANW: $340B by 2030) are typically far larger than third-party segment estimates (~$120–150B for the categories PANW competes in). Why it matters: use the narrow figure for penetration math (~8%) and the broad one for optionality.

Runtime Security / AI Gateway

Security enforced on live traffic and actions (prompts, model calls, agent tool use) at execution time, rather than on static code or configuration

McKinsey's prescription for the collapsed defense window; Palo Alto delivers it through Prisma AIRS, with the acquired Portkey AI gateway routing every model interaction through a policy-enforcement point. Why it matters: it is the fastest-growing product line in company history ($100M ARR in four quarters) and the strategic reason the McKinsey report names Palo Alto.
